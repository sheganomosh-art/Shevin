## The only thing that matters this week

### The writing bar

Before every sentence you write, ask:

> "Can a tired 26-year-old Kenyan reading this on a Tecno phone after work understand this without rereading it?"

If no: rewrite it.

### The simplification test

Take every sentence you write and try to cut it in half.

```
BEFORE: "The NSE facilitates capital formation through 
         public participation in equity markets."

AFTER:  "The NSE is where people buy small ownership 
         pieces of Kenyan companies."

BEFORE: "Diversification mitigates concentration risk 
         across asset classes."

AFTER:  "Don't put all your money in one company."

BEFORE: "The Capital Markets Authority provides 
         regulatory oversight of listed securities."

AFTER:  "The CMA is the government body that makes 
         sure the NSE plays by the rules."
```

The simpler version is always better for your audience. Always.

### The trust test

After writing each paragraph, ask:

> "Does this sound like someone trying to educate me, or sell me something?"

If there's any doubt: remove the sentence causing the doubt.

## The testing protocol

When Lesson 1 draft is done, do this exactly:

**Who to test with:**
- Employed Kenyan, 22–35
- Not a developer
- Not already interested in investing
- Ideally: skeptical about financial products

**How to test:**
- Send them the Google Doc or sit with them
- Say: "Read this. Tell me what's confusing."
- Then: be completely silent
- Do not explain anything
- Do not defend anything
- Do not guide them

**What to watch:**
- Where do they slow down?
- Where do they reread?
- Where do they look up?
- Where do they stop?

Every one of those moments is product truth. Write it down. Rewrite the lesson. Repeat.

**The bar:** They can answer 4 of 5 quiz questions correctly without you explaining anything. If they can't, the lesson failed — not the reader.

## Mwalimu — the constraint

One sentence on what Mwalimu should be:

> A patient teaching assistant who knows which lesson you're on and can answer questions about it clearly.

One sentence on what it should never become:

> An investing genius who answers any financial question from any context.

The more general-purpose it gets, the more expensive, dangerous, and unfocused it becomes. Every time you're tempted to make it smarter, make it more constrained instead.

## The real questions — the only ones

Until 50 users have completed the course, the only questions worth asking are:

```
1. Do users trust it?
2. Do users finish lessons?
3. Do users understand the material?
4. Do users return voluntarily?
```

Not: "How do we scale?" Not: "What's the monetization model?" Not: "Should we add a forum?"

Those questions become relevant when the answer to all four above is yes.
